PU Leather vs Genuine Leather: Which Should You Source?

The choice between PU leather vs genuine leather decides your landed cost, your reject rate, and how your finished goods behave after two years of daily use. As a PU and synthetic leather factory shipping roll goods to furniture, automotive, and bag brands, we get this question on almost every first inquiry. This page lays out the technical and commercial trade-offs the way we explain them to buyers on a factory call, with the numbers, standards, and order terms you need to brief your sourcing team. For broader background on the material, our PU leather sourcing guide covers grades, constructions, and testing in more depth.

Genuine leather is a tanned animal hide, sold by the hide or by the square foot, with natural grain, brands, and thickness variation. PU (polyurethane) synthetic leather is a coated textile: a polyurethane skin laid over a knit, woven, or non-woven base, sold by the linear meter on rolls of fixed width. They look similar on a finished sofa or seat, but they source, price, and certify very differently.

How the two materials are built and measured

Genuine leather starts as a wet hide that is tanned (usually chrome or vegetable), split, dyed, and finished. Because it follows the shape of the animal, a hide carries belly looseness, neck wrinkles, insect marks, and a usable yield that often runs 75-85% after cutting around defects. You buy area, not length, and you accept grade variation hide to hide.

PU leather is engineered to a target spec and produced in continuous rolls, typically 54-58" (137-147 cm) wide, in run lengths of 30-50 m per roll. Total thickness lands between 0.6 mm and 1.8 mm depending on the application, with surface weight of roughly 320-1100 g/m². Because every meter is made to the same recipe, batch-to-batch consistency is far tighter than a stack of hides, which is exactly why high-volume cut-and-sew lines favor it.

Which one performs better for your application?

Performance is not a single number. It depends on the abrasion the surface will see, how often it gets wet or cleaned, and whether your buyer expects a true hide. Here is how the comparison actually shakes out by use case.

  • Abrasion and high-traffic seating: A 1.0-1.4 mm furniture-grade PU rated 50,000-100,000 Martindale (ISO 5470-2) holds up well on a sofa. For airports, hospitality, or fleet automotive, microfiber PU reaches 100,000-150,000 cycles and matches or beats mid-grade hide.
  • Water and cleaning: PU is water-resistant by construction and wipes clean with a damp cloth; genuine leather absorbs liquids unless heavily finished and needs conditioning. For details on moisture behavior, see our notes on whether PU leather is durable and waterproof.
  • Breathability and feel: Genuine leather breathes and develops a patina that some premium buyers want. PU is more uniform and, in microfiber grades, very close in hand feel, but it does not patina.
  • Hydrolysis and lifespan: Lower-grade PU can break down in humid heat; specify a hydrolysis-resistant grade tested to ISO 1419 (typically 5-7 weeks jungle test) for tropical export markets. Quality hide does not hydrolyze but is sensitive to dryness and mold.
  • Cut efficiency: Roll goods cut nesting-friendly with low waste; hides force you to plan around defects, which raises real per-part cost beyond the sticker price.

If your spec demands a top-coat that survives UV and flex without cracking, that is a coating-formulation question on the PU side and a finish question on the hide side, so always confirm the test report rather than the marketing grade. Cost, MOQ, and lead time for an export order

Commercially, the two supply chains barely resemble each other. Genuine leather pricing swings with raw hide markets, tannery capacity, and currency, and quality hides can cost several times more per usable square foot than PU. You also carry the yield loss: if 20% of a hide is unusable, your effective cost climbs accordingly.

PU leather is quoted per meter and is far more stable. Standard coated PU typically carries a minimum order quantity around 1,000 m per color/article, while microfiber PU runs closer to ~300 m MOQ because the base is more specialized. Stock colors ship faster; custom embossing, color matching, and special backings extend timing. A typical FOB lead time runs 15-25 days after sample and PI approval, versus the longer and less predictable cycle of sorting and grading hides.

Compliance and documentation buyers will ask for

Both materials face chemical and safety regulation, but the paperwork differs. For PU sold into the EU and North America, buyers commonly require REACH (SVHC), RoHS, and OEKO-TEX testing, plus California Prop 65 screening for restricted substances. Automotive and furniture programs add flame-retardancy and VOC requirements on top. We supply test reports against the standard the buyer names rather than generic claims.

  • Chemical compliance: REACH and RoHS substance limits, OEKO-TEX for skin-contact articles, Prop 65 for the US market.
  • Physical testing: abrasion (ISO 5470-2 / ASTM D4157), peel strength (ISO 2411), hydrolysis (ISO 1419), and colorfastness to rubbing and light.
  • Traceability: genuine leather increasingly needs tannery and origin documentation; PU needs article codes, batch numbers, and the coating/base spec on the technical data sheet.

One practical note: PU and PVC are not the same, and confusing them on a spec sheet causes failed audits and rejected shipments. If your buyer wrote "vinyl" or "faux leather" without detail, settle it early using our breakdown of PU leather vs PVC leather differences before you lock the bill of materials.

So which should you source?

Choose genuine leather when your brand positioning, price point, and buyer expectation specifically demand a real hide, and when you can absorb yield loss, lot variation, and price volatility. The patina, breathability, and perceived prestige are real advantages in premium segments.

Choose PU synthetic leather when you need consistent rolls, predictable per-meter cost, repeatable color across reorders, water resistance, and faster lead times, which describes most furniture, automotive, and bag programs at scale. The PU leather vs genuine leather decision usually comes down to whether the project rewards natural character or production consistency.
